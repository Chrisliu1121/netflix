The Economics of Content

Where did Netflix's $20 billion go?

Most of it bought productions that have not been released yet. On the historical pattern, the cost reaches the income statement two to three quarters later. The squeeze looks milder than in 2022, so slower margin expansion is more likely than a repeat of the 2023 earnings decline.

The Gap Between Growth Engines

Can advertising catch up as the gains from price increases fade?

In the US and Canada, revenue growth in Q2 and Q3 2026 sat barely above the March price rise. Advertising is about 5.3% of revenue and still too small to take over. Europe, with the largest membership base and lower revenue per member, looks like the likeliest source of the next wave.

Waiting for the Next Breakup

If Netflix is so far ahead, why do content owners keep losing money instead of licensing to it?

Each company maximizes something different, so nobody surrenders. Rights keep fragmenting while the entry point consolidates around Amazon and YouTube. Netflix can afford to wait at the content layer, but it needs to act at the entry point by becoming a selective aggregator.

PART III
Two analyses in brief

Spending and expense move on different clocks

Content additions are the money Netflix commits to new titles. Amortization is when that money becomes expense. Additions fell 25% in 2023 while revenue reaccelerated, and amortization barely grew in 2024. That gap explains much of the recent margin expansion.

Key findings

Ten findings across the series

1

Spending and growth rarely move together

Content additions fell 25% in 2023, yet revenue reaccelerated in the second half. Paid sharing, price rises and the ad tier did most of the work, and those gains cannot be repeated indefinitely.

PART IQ4 2023 growth 12.5%
2

Some recent margins were borrowed from the strike years

With investment paused in 2023, amortization fell from 45.0% to 35.2% of revenue and the trailing operating margin rose from 16.8% to 31.3%. That tailwind is now ending.

PART ILag of 2 to 3 quarters
3

Renting and owning differ after amortization ends

A licensed title leaves when its rights expire. An owned original can stay on the service after its book value reaches zero, and the balance sheet does not show that value.

PART ILibrary share of viewing about 63%
4

Viewing hours matter most for advertising

Hours do not drive subscription revenue in a straight line, but they are the first term in the advertising equation. Revenue per hour grew about 11 to 12%, and amortization per hour about 7 to 8%.

PART IHours up 2% in H1 2026
5

US growth now rests on pricing

The Standard plan rose 29% from 2024 to March 2026. Rival ad tiers priced between $8.99 and $12.99 anchor the entry price, and the market's growth expectations increasingly set the timing of each rise.

PART IIUCAN is 43.5% of revenue
6

Live events appear to pay for themselves

Rough estimates put the payback on the fourth-quarter live events of 2024 and 2025 at six to twelve months. The larger risk is whether the members they bring in stay after the next price rise.

PART IIJapan WBC: record sign-up day
7

Who surrenders depends on what each company maximizes

The first streaming war was a prisoner's dilemma. Now Disney maximizes the value of its IP, Comcast the return on a content portfolio, and Skydance the strategic vision of its controlling family.

PART IIIPeacock losses near $10B, 2021 to 2025
8

Skydance is still fighting the first war

After buying Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery, net debt is about 6.4 times 2027 EBITDA before synergies, and interest takes nearly half of EBITDA. Financial pressure is already pushing it to license its library.

PART III3x leverage target by 2029
9

Sports are content that never surrenders

Leagues slice their rights to keep many buyers bidding. In the 2028 to 2030 auctions, Netflix is the only buyer with a real choice, and a credible outside bid is enough to move renewal prices.

PART IIINBA deal 2.6x prior annual value
10

The new battlefield is the entry point

Netflix is the strongest destination but not yet an aggregator. The series argues it should become a selective one, and the TF1 integration in France is a first step management wants to repeat.

PART IIIFox bought Roku for about $22B
The strategic recommendation

Wait at the content layer. Act at the entry point.

  • Rivals are already becoming suppliers. At the content layer only Skydance is worth waiting for, as it works toward its 2029 leverage target.
  • Entry points and sports rights are hard to win back once taken, so Netflix should become a selective aggregator that keeps billing and the customer relationship.
  • The 2028 to 2030 sports auctions are the moment to change stance. Even a losing bid raises renewal costs for indebted rivals.
  • If no new partner or add-on appears within two to three years, the view was wrong.
The investment view

A question of price rather than safety.

  • At a 10% cost of equity, the share price implies free cash flow growth of about 12 to 15% a year for five years, around 13.5% at the midpoint.
  • The series' base case is 14% or more. Each point of growth over the first five years is worth about 6% of value.
  • The downside needs two things at once: subscription price rises stop and advertising growth slows sharply.
  • Return on invested capital is about 38 to 43%, far above a 9 to 11% cost of capital.

Stated limits

What the series cannot see

  • Netflix does not disclose advertising revenue by region, so Europe as the next ad engine remains an inference.
  • Plan mix is undisclosed, so the UCAN split between pricing, members and ads is a bounded estimate rather than an exact decomposition.
  • The lag of two to three quarters is a historical estimate from a short sample, since large-scale original production is only five or six years old.
  • Live event costs and third-party viewing shares come from press reports and Parrot Analytics, whose methodology is not public.
  • The aggregation view has a deadline: if no new partner, sports add-on or US streaming add-on appears within two to three years, the view was wrong.
